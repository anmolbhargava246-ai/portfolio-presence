Project 01 · Product research · Enterprise B2B SaaS (ITSM, mobile)

The brief said validate. The evidence said prioritise.

An evaluative study that moved the team from “do these four features work?” to “which capabilities matter on mobile, for whom, and why?”

Enterprise B2B SaaSResearch strategyCross-functional partnership
TL;DR
Space
Enterprise ITSM, mobile
Product
Four new on-call scheduling capabilities in Jira Mobile, during the Opsgenie → Jira consolidation
Method
Moderated task-based usability with role-based scenarios (SEQ and time taken, directional) + workflow interviews
Sample
12 participants across 5 countries — 6 Engineering Managers, 6 Engineers; Opsgenie users and people on competitor tools; internal pilot first
Partnered with
Product, Design and Research leadership
Outcome
Evidence to prioritise Overrides for the mobile roadmap; workflow and role inputs for Design and Research
Strategic shift
“Do these four features work?” → “Which capabilities matter on mobile, for whom, and in what context?”
The problem
  • Atlassian was consolidating Opsgenie capabilities into Jira; on-call scheduling was coming to Jira Mobile
  • On-call scheduling decides who responds to incidents, and when; Overrides let a manager replace an assignment when circumstances change
  • Four newly designed capabilities to evaluate before rollout: daily schedule, monthly schedule, card view, overrides
  • The brief: do these features work, and are they appropriate for mobile?
The question underneath
The brief

Do these four features work, and are they appropriate for mobile?

What emerged during the sessions

Feature usability alone wasn’t enough. Which scheduling capabilities actually matter on mobile, for whom, and in what context?

The lens that followed

Feature evaluation → workflow understanding → mobile criticality → product prioritisation

How it was run
  • Three stakeholder needs in one study: Product (migration and mobile prioritisation), Design (do the four features work?), Research (roles, workflows, journey maps, personas)
  • Moderated, task-based usability: role-based scenarios across the four capabilities — completion, interaction problems, hesitation, ease and time taken
  • Workflow interviews in every session: when would this capability actually be valuable in someone’s work?
  • Two roles by design — managers and engineers share the workflow, not the responsibilities
  • 12 participants across 5 countries; Opsgenie users and people on competitor tools; internal pilot before the main sessions
  • SEQ and time taken treated as directional — depth and role coverage, not statistical representation
What we found
Role shaped what mattered in the same workflow

Engineers focused on their own schedule, their own responsibilities and alerts. Managers needed team-level visibility, team schedules, roster information and the ability to manage assignments.

Engineers went to Alerts first

4 of 6 opened Alerts before anything else. The schedule gets checked; alerts are the day.

Overrides solved a time-sensitive mobile job

All six Engineering Managers named Overrides the most important scheduling capability on the go: someone becomes unavailable, a shift changes, a different person needs assigning — often away from a laptop.

“I can handle schedule changes without having to get on my laptop. That’s huge when something comes up.”

— Engineering Manager
Usability said how; workflow said where and for whom

The task data showed how well each interaction worked. The workflow context showed where that interaction actually mattered, and to whom.

The key insight
Role + mobile context > feature usability alone

Usability testing showed how well each interaction worked. Workflow context showed where, and for whom, it actually mattered — which is what Product needed in order to prioritise.

  • All six managers: Overrides the most important capability on the go
  • 4 of 6 engineers opened Alerts first
  • Managers wanted the team’s roster; engineers their own schedule
  • Schedule changes are time-sensitive and happen away from the laptop
What changed
  • Product: evidence to prioritise Overrides for the mobile roadmap, and around mobile-critical capabilities more broadly
  • Product: supported thinking on Integrations and Alerts; unlocked follow-up research
  • Design: interaction friction exposed and the scheduling experience refined before broader launch — lower rollout risk
  • Research: real workflow differences added to personas, richer inputs for journey mapping, a stronger research panel
  • The question the team took forward: which capabilities matter on mobile, for whom, and why
How I worked with the team
  • Three stakeholder needs — Product, Design, Research — balanced in one study rather than three research efforts
  • Insights soft-shared and sparred on before the readout — no big reveal
  • Delivered as verbatims, Loom clips, and how-might-we prompts design could act on
  • Product owned prioritisation; my part was the evidence and the wider lens
What I’d do differently today
  • Frame the decision earlier: what are we actually deciding — what matters on mobile, for whom, why, and what evidence would change the prioritisation
  • Use existing evidence first: desktop usage, product analytics, support signals, previous research — find the biggest uncertainty, then research that
  • Run recruitment, pilot, secondary research, guide refinement and stakeholder observation in parallel — reach the decision faster without reducing rigour
Why this matters in enterprise
  • The unit of analysis is the workflow, not the screen — two roles shared one scheduling system and needed different things from it
  • A tactical brief can carry a bigger decision — the job is to notice it during the research and widen the lens without overstepping
  • I don’t need to research everything; I need to find where more evidence would change the decision
  • Research pays off at the decision layer: which capabilities get mobile priority, for whom, and why
Takeaway

The brief asked whether four features worked. The useful answer was which ones mattered on mobile, for whom, and why — and that is the question I’d ask first today.
