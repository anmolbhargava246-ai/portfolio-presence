Project 01 · Product research · Enterprise B2B SaaS (ITSM, mobile)

The brief said faster. The evidence said predictable.

An evaluative study that moved the team from “how do we make scheduling faster?” to “is scheduling the right problem for mobile?”

Strategic shift
“How do we make scheduling faster on mobile?” → “Which parts of the on-call workflow does mobile actually carry?”
The problem

An on-call engineer gets paged at 2am. Whether that moment goes well depends on unglamorous things: knowing you're actually on shift, an override that took effect, a schedule that hasn't quietly changed. In enterprise incident management, ambiguity is operational risk.

The company was consolidating a standalone incident-management product into its flagship ITSM platform — an OKR-level bet, with mobile as part of it. New on-call scheduling features were headed for the mobile app, on the logic that faster, easier scheduling would help drive adoption.

Inside that logic sat an assumption worth naming: that scheduling was what mobile on-call users needed most.

The question underneath

Designs were in flight, so the evaluative question was urgent. But prior research argued against stopping there: earlier studies showed most users opened the mobile app to acknowledge and close alerts — not to browse schedules. So the study carried two questions at once.

The evaluative question

Can engineers and managers find, understand, and operate the new scheduling components?

The strategic question

Is scheduling actually where mobile earns its place in the on-call workflow?

How it was run

A deliberately tactical method, run strictly, matched to decision speed. Two cohorts mirrored the workflow's two actors — engineers, who live their own schedule, and managers, who are accountable for the team's — because in enterprise scheduling those are different jobs, not different demographics.

  • Stakeholder interviews with design and PM before writing the discussion guide
  • Screener and prototype piloted with internal on-call engineers
  • Sessions observed live by the triad, tagged in Dovetail as they happened
  • One participant excluded when their session contradicted their screener — 11 of 12 analysed
  • Journey mapping declared out of scope up front; claims stayed inside the tested boundary
What we found
Engineers kept drifting toward alerts

Asked to check their day, four of six engineers went to the alerts tab and called it the most useful part of the app. Schedule admin is occasional; alerts are daily.

The workflow runs on predictability, not speed

The one feature built for speed — taking a shift “for an hour” in one tap — met shrugs: schedules are set well in advance. Users wanted to see weeks ahead, not save seconds.

Learned patterns beat novel ones

Participants wanted to keep colour associations they'd already built, and succeeded on the override paths that matched expectations while the novel one confused. In a six-or-seven-tool ecosystem, consistency outperforms cleverness.

One screen, two different jobs

Managers called the “my schedules” framing noise — they never take call. What they reached for was whole-team visibility: coverage, gaps, who can fill a slot.

“As a manager, I am never gonna fill in for anybody's absence. I am not gonna sit and take incident calls.”

— P7, engineering manager
The key insight
Predictability + learnability > simply reducing task time

Mobile's value wasn't finishing scheduling tasks faster — it was knowing what's coming and trusting patterns already learned. A read across converging signals, not a measured effect; but every signal pointed this way, and none pointed toward speed.

  • Schedules set well in advance — the one-tap speed feature met shrugs
  • Users asked to see weeks ahead, here and in prior studies
  • Colour used as memory for people and shifts
  • Familiar override paths succeeded; the novel one confused
What changed
  • The team's question reframed: from “make scheduling faster” to “invest where mobile carries the workflow” — overrides, alert awareness, team visibility
  • Manager view recommended to be rebuilt around team visibility, not personal schedules
  • Card-view customisation deprioritised — the weakest task, and participants said so themselves
  • 4 design changes shipped, simplifying navigation and interaction patterns; 2 features added to the roadmap
  • +15% satisfaction and ~+10% adoption post-launch — the team's post-launch measures, not this study's claim
How I worked with the team

A triad throughout — researcher, designer, PM, with engineering close. Insights were soft-shared and sparred on before the readout, then packaged as verbatims, Loom clips, and how-might-we prompts so design could act on them directly. Decisions stayed with product; research's job was making sure they were made against the workflow users actually have, not the one the roadmap assumed.

Why this matters in enterprise

Enterprise products are workflows before they are screens: multiple actors, tool ecosystems, competing priorities. A feature request is rarely the underlying problem — and research earns its keep by checking the team is solving the right one while there's still time to redirect.

Takeaway

Speed is easy to measure, which makes it easy to over-value. This workflow ran on predictability and learned patterns — and research's job was to notice which problem deserved the screen.
