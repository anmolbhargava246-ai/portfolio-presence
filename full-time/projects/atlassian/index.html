Project 01 · Product research · Enterprise B2B SaaS (ITSM, mobile)

The brief said faster. The evidence said predictable.

An evaluative study of mobile on-call scheduling that changed the team's question — from “how do we make scheduling faster?” to “is scheduling the right thing to optimise for mobile at all?”

The product problem

An engineering manager pages an on-call engineer at 2am because a critical incident just landed. Whether that moment goes well depends on unglamorous things: the engineer knowing they're actually on shift, an override that took effect the way both parties expected, a schedule that looks the same today as it did yesterday. In enterprise incident management, ambiguity isn't a cosmetic flaw — it's operational risk.

This study sat inside a company-level bet: consolidating a standalone incident-management product into the flagship ITSM platform, with a top-line OKR attached. Mobile was part of that bet. The design team had built new on-call scheduling features for the mobile app — daily and monthly schedule views, card-view customisation, overrides — reasoning that easier, faster access to schedules on the go would strengthen the case for the consolidated app.

Inside that reasoning lived an assumption worth naming: that scheduling was what mobile on-call users needed most, and that making it quicker would translate into adoption.

The research question

The tactical question was real and urgent: can on-call engineers and managers find, understand, and operate the new scheduling components? Designs were in flight, and the team needed that answer at the speed of their decisions.

But prior research argued for a second question underneath it. Earlier studies of on-call tooling had found that most users opened the mobile app to acknowledge and close alerts — only some used it to check who was on call. If that still held, polishing scheduling might improve a screen without moving the outcome the team actually cared about.

So the study carried two questions at once. The evaluative one: do these components work? And the strategic one: where in the on-call workflow does mobile actually earn its place — and is scheduling that place?

The research strategy

The method was deliberately tactical: 90-minute remote moderated usability sessions, task-based, with a Single Ease Question rating after each task. A leaner method matched to decision speed, with the rigour invested in how it was run — and in refusing to look at the feature in isolation. Every task was read against the workflow around it: what participants reached for first, what they ignored, what they said their day actually looks like.

The sample mirrored the workflow rather than a single persona: on-call engineers, who live their own schedule and add overrides, and engineering managers and team leads, who plan the team's coverage and change it. In enterprise scheduling these are structurally different jobs — one is subject to the schedule, the other is accountable for it — and collapsing them into one “user” would have blurred exactly the differences that mattered. All participants handled on-call tooling in their day-to-day work, across dedicated incident-management platforms, internal tools, and manual schedules.

Before fieldwork: stakeholder interviews with the designer and PM to absorb the intent behind the flows, and a pilot of both screener and prototype with internal on-call engineers. During fieldwork: sessions observed live by the triad and note-takers, and tagged in Dovetail as they happened, so synthesis built continuously instead of arriving as a cliff at the end. One recruited participant cleared the screener but contradicted it in session; their data was excluded, leaving 11 of 12. Small samples only hold up if you're strict about what counts.

Scope was explicit too. Journey mapping was declared out of scope up front — this study tested scheduling designs, and its claims stay inside that boundary.

What we learned

On the surface, the evaluation passed. Every participant completed the daily-schedule, monthly-schedule, and override tasks. Overrides rated easiest (SEQ 5.3 of 7) and drew the most unprompted enthusiasm. The one clear usability failure was card-view customisation: 7 of 11 completed it, 4 never found the settings, and 3 said the feature added no value regardless.

If the question had stayed “is this usable?”, the study would have ended there. The more consequential evidence was in what participants did and said around the tasks.

Engineers kept drifting toward alerts

Observed Asked to check “what their day looks like”, four of six engineers went to the alerts tab, and described alerts as the most useful part of the app in daily work. One asked whether they could start responding to an alert — preliminary investigation — from the phone itself.

“Having the alerts at the very top and the very home screen — that's probably the primary usefulness.”

— P9, on-call engineer

The read For engineers, mobile's job in on-call work is awareness and response. Schedule administration is occasional; alerts are daily. A scheduling-first mobile strategy had that emphasis inverted.

The workflow runs on predictability, not speed

Observed The feature built for speed — taking a shift “for an hour” in one tap — got the most ambivalent reception in the study. Participants pointed out that schedules are set well in advance; those who liked the option mostly asked to customise its duration. Prior research had the same shape: users wanted to see their coming weeks so they could plan ahead. Nobody was asking for scheduling to be quicker.

The read On-call scheduling is a system for eliminating surprises. Participants optimised for knowing what's coming, not for shaving seconds off actions they take occasionally.

Learned patterns beat novel ones

Observed Participants leaned on visual memory, wanting to keep the associations they'd already built between colours and people. Where the interface matched expectations — two of the three paths for adding an override — everyone succeeded. The third path, which behaved unlike what its control suggested, mostly confused the few who tried it.

“I usually tend to get comfortable with seeing a certain colour, associating that with a certain user.”

— P9, on-call engineer

The read These teams already juggle six or seven tools — one manager listed them from memory. In an ecosystem that taxes attention this heavily, consistency and learnability are worth more than cleverness; every unfamiliar pattern spends memory that's already spoken for.

One screen was quietly serving two different jobs

Observed Managers were blunt that the app's framing didn't fit them: a “my schedules” tab is noise for someone who never takes call. What they reached for, repeatedly, was whole-team visibility — everyone's coverage in one place, who could fill an empty slot, ideally with project context attached.

“As a manager, I am never gonna fill in for anybody's absence. I am not gonna sit and take incident calls.”

— P7, engineering manager

The read Engineers are subject to the schedule; managers are accountable for it. One view designed for both actors meant the manager half of the workflow had no real home.

The key insight
Predictability + learnability > simply reducing task time

What made the mobile experience valuable wasn't completing scheduling tasks faster. It was knowing what's coming, and trusting patterns already learned. That's a read across converging qualitative signals — task behaviour, verbatims, and prior studies — not a measured effect. But every signal pointed the same direction, and none of them pointed toward speed.

  • Schedules are set well in advance — the one-tap speed feature met shrugs
  • Users asked to see weeks ahead so they could plan, in this study and prior ones
  • Colour was used as memory: participants wanted learned associations preserved
  • Override paths that matched expectations succeeded; the novel one confused
How the research changed the product conversation

The readout didn't argue the designs were bad — mostly, they worked. It argued the question was too narrow. Instead of “how do we make scheduling faster on mobile?”, the evidence supported asking “which parts of on-call work does mobile actually carry?” — and the answer clustered around overrides, alert awareness, and team-level visibility rather than schedule browsing and cosmetic customisation.

Concretely, the recommendations: rebuild the manager view around team information rather than a personal-schedule frame; fold card detail into tap-to-expand instead of a separate settings toggle; add on-demand detail to the monthly view; make the one-tap shift takeover duration-customisable. And one deprioritisation: card-view customisation — the weakest task in the study — was flagged as low priority, which participants had said themselves in nearly as many words.

Cross-functional collaboration

The project ran on a triad — researcher, designer, PM — with the engineering manager close. I interviewed the designer and PM before writing the discussion guide, so the study tested their actual intent rather than my reading of it. They watched sessions from the observer panel, and insights were soft-shared and sparred on before the report was final — partly for accuracy, partly because conclusions people help shape travel further than conclusions they're handed.

The evidence was packaged to be used, not admired: verbatims and Loom clips so users' own voices did the persuading, findings framed as problems and how-might-we prompts so design could pick them up directly, and a recorded shareback so the insights reached people who weren't in the room.

The decisions stayed where they belonged — with product and design. Research's job was to make sure those decisions were made looking at the workflow users actually have, not the one the roadmap assumed.

Outcome

Being precise about what happened next:

  • Directly observed: all core scheduling tasks were operable; overrides was the standout (easiest, most valued); card-view customisation failed on findability and perceived value.
  • Recommended: a manager view rebuilt around team visibility, progressive detail in schedule views, and a duration-customisable shift takeover.
  • Deprioritised: further investment in card-view customisation.
  • Shipped by the team: 4 design changes simplifying navigation and interaction patterns, and 2 features added to the product roadmap.
  • Post-launch: +15% user satisfaction and ~+10% feature adoption.
  • Ongoing: research partnered with design to track how the insights played into adoption after the consolidation.

Two caveats on that ledger, deliberately kept: the satisfaction and adoption figures are post-launch measures, not outputs of this study — and the study's claim is to the reprioritisation, not the numbers.

Why this matters for enterprise UX research

Enterprise products are workflows before they are screens. The useful contributions here came from treating the study that way: two actors with structurally different jobs, an ecosystem of six or seven tools, schedules that encode a team's agreements about risk. Feature-level usability was the entry point, not the destination.

A feature request is rarely the underlying problem. Participants asked for colour customisation; the need underneath was continuity of learned patterns. The team asked for faster scheduling; the workflow ran on predictability.

The highest-leverage thing research did here wasn't finding usability issues. It was helping the team check whether they were solving the right problem while there was still time to redirect — and in enterprise B2B, where roadmaps carry OKR-level commitments, that check is worth more than polish.

Takeaway

Speed is easy to measure, which makes it easy to over-value. This workflow ran on predictability and learned patterns — and research's job was to notice which problem actually deserved the screen.
